## 500-word summary

Large Language Models represent a fundamentally different form of intelligence that operates through distinct mechanical processes unlike human cognition. To leverage LLMs effectively, practitioners must understand three core mechanics that govern their behavior: training data composition, context window limitations, and the tendency toward hallucination. The first fundamental concept is training data. An LLM's entire knowledge universe consists of the massive dataset used during its training phase. This data is historical by nature, meaning the model has no inherent awareness of current events or recent developments. All responses generated by the model are based on patterns extracted from past information, not on real-time data or ongoing events. This creates an important constraint: LLMs cannot answer questions about anything that occurred after their training cutoff date, and they have no mechanism for learning about new developments between interactions. The second critical concept is the context window, which functions as the model's short-term memory. During any interaction, an LLM can only reference information that exists within its current context window—the conversation or document currently being processed. These windows vary significantly across different models, ranging from a few thousand words to over a million words in some advanced systems. However, there is a hard boundary: once information moves outside this window, whether through scrolling, starting a new conversation, or any other means, the model effectively forgets that information entirely. This means LLMs cannot remember user preferences from one session to the next, cannot accumulate knowledge about a company's strategy over time, and cannot build on previous interactions. Each conversation begins from a blank slate, confined solely to what exists within the current context window. The third concept to understand is hallucination. LLMs are designed fundamentally as next-word prediction engines—they generate text by calculating the most statistically probable word to follow any given sequence. This design means that when a model encounters a question it cannot accurately answer, it will still generate a response that sounds authoritative and plausible. These fabricated responses can appear completely convincing while containing factual errors or complete inventions. This is why the term hallucination is used in the AI field to describe this phenomenon. These three mechanics—training data limitations, context window constraints, and hallucination tendencies—form the foundation for understanding LLM capabilities and limitations. For strategic deployment, this knowledge enables the architecture of more sophisticated solutions. Organizations can implement systems that feed models real-time data to overcome training data constraints, use external memory databases to maintain state across conversations, and build guardrails to detect and prevent hallucinations. This understanding separates amateur users from strategic operators who can transform what begins as a consumer tool into a powerful enterprise asset.

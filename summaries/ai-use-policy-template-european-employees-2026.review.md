## 500-word summary

The core problem this article addresses is that most AI use policies fail not because they are incorrect but because they are written for lawyers rather than for the employees who must live with them daily. The guide provides a practical AI use policy template specifically designed for European SMEs that addresses EU AI Act and GDPR requirements while remaining accessible to non-legal staff. The policy structure covers six essential sections: Purpose and Scope establishes who the policy applies to and why it exists, providing organizational context that helps employees understand the policy's intent beyond mere compliance language. Approved Tools and Request Process provides a clear list of permitted tools with specific use cases and a formal request procedure for new tools, addressing the common confusion employees face when trying to determine what is permissible. Data Handling Rules defines which data categories may and may not be processed through AI tools, explaining that personal data entered into tools without a Data Processing Agreement creates GDPR compliance exposure for the organization. This section addresses the practical reality that employees may not understand the distinction between internal data processing and third-party AI service processing, making clear explanations essential. AI-Generated Content and Transparency establishes human review requirements for consequential outputs and disclosure obligations under EU AI Act Article 50, recognizing that while AI can assist productivity, final decisions affecting business outcomes require human judgment. Incidents and Escalation defines what constitutes an AI-related incident and provides a clear reporting pathway, ensuring employees know exactly how to act when something goes wrong rather than hesitating due to uncertainty. Employee Responsibilities Summary provides a one-page checklist for easy reference, acknowledging that policies are only effective if employees can quickly refresh their understanding. The article emphasizes that policies must explain the reasoning behind rules so employees understand why restrictions exist, not just what is prohibited—this principle of explaining the why rather than just the what represents a fundamental shift from traditional compliance documentation. For approved tool lists, the guidance states clarity is the primary design goal—lists should be detailed enough that a new employee can determine within 60 seconds whether a tool is approved and for what purpose, recognizing that overly complex approval systems simply encourage shadow IT usage. The article also addresses personal AI subscriptions, recommending that personal accounts without Data Processing Agreements are not approved tools regardless of who pays for them, addressing the increasingly common scenario of employees using personal ChatGPT or Claude subscriptions for work tasks. Effective rollout requires launch briefings connecting policy to real scenarios that employees recognize from their daily work, employee acknowledgments creating an evidence trail for compliance demonstration, periodic quarterly reviews of approved tool lists to account for the rapidly changing AI tool landscape, and integration into new hire onboarding processes to establish expectations from day one rather than treating policy review as an afterthought.

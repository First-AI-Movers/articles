## 500-word summary

The article argues that most companies fail with AI not because the models are weak, but because nobody owns the gap between business reality and technical execution. The absence of a dedicated AI transformation lead is where budgets disappear, pilots stall, and teams quietly return to old workflows. The article contends that if companies are serious about using AI to improve how they build, operate, and deliver value, they do not just need another engineer, another tool, or another workshop. They need someone who can identify where AI creates real leverage, translate that into something the technical team can build, and then make sure the organization actually adopts it. First AI Movers helps organizations establish the function that sits between AI ambition and business execution, focusing on five key outcomes. First, they map where AI can create leverage across functions like operations, finance, customer success, marketing, and product. Second, they help define clear technical briefs that specify the problem, context, constraints, desired behavior, and success metrics. Third, they make adoption part of the work itself rather than an afterthought, helping teams shift from manual execution to supervision and oversight. Fourth, they provide leadership with a roadmap that sequences initiatives to build momentum without creating chaos. Fifth, they help the organization build actual AI capability, not just curiosity. The article emphasizes that this role requires someone who understands workflows, product thinking, technical constraints, human resistance, organizational priorities, and the difference between a useful pilot and a costly distraction. This is the bridge role that companies need to transform from people doing all the work themselves to people directing systems that can do parts of the work with them. The reasoning behind this is that AI projects fail not at the model development stage but at the handover between business and engineering, where vague requirements meet technical constraints, where pilot enthusiasm meets operational reality, and where adoption metrics are never defined. The article suggests that this role serves as the connective tissue that keeps AI initiatives aligned with business outcomes throughout the project lifecycle, translating between the language of business value and the language of technical feasibility. Without this role, organizations risk investing in AI systems that are technically impressive but operationally irrelevant, or that solve problems that do not actually exist in the day-to-day work of the teams meant to use them. The decision criteria for evaluating whether this role is needed include whether AI budgets are producing measurable business impact, whether pilots are progressing beyond the experimental phase, and whether technical teams are building solutions that business stakeholders recognize as addressing their actual needs. The risks of not filling this role include continued investment in AI initiatives that stall at the pilot stage, organizational fatigue with AI promises, and the opportunity cost of not capturing AI's potential to transform how work gets done. The operating implications are that companies must treat AI adoption as an organizational change management challenge rather than a purely technical implementation, and they must staff for the translation layer between business and technology rather than assuming it will emerge naturally from existing roles.

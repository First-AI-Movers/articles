Most European SMEs fall under the deployer classification under Article 50 rather than the provider classification under Article 53, meaning they face transparency obligations rather than the full weight of model provider requirements. This distinction represents the most common compliance mistake that legal leads and CTOs make when preparing for the regulation, as they conflate provider obligations with deployer obligations and assume requirements that do not apply to their organization. The article provides a practical six-point compliance checklist for SME deployers. First, transparency notices must be added for AI-generated content, presented clearly at the point of interaction rather than buried in terms and conditions. Second, companies need to register which GPAI models they use, including model name, version, provider, use case, and whether output is customer-facing or internal. Third, human oversight arrangements should be documented for each AI-assisted workflow, including who reviews outputs and what criteria govern human intervention. Fourth, vendor contracts should be reviewed for Article 25 obligations and GDPR compliance, ensuring vendors can confirm their own EU AI Act compliance posture. Fifth, watermarking and machine-readable disclosure is required for AI-generated images, video, or audio that could be mistaken for authentic content. Sixth, data governance must be established for any fine-tuning or customization using company or customer data. The article clarifies what most SMEs are not required to do. Companies do not need to comply with Article 53 unless they develop and place GPAI models on the market. They do not need to publish technical documentation about model training, compute thresholds, or systemic risk assessments. They do not need to register in the EU database unless deploying high-risk AI systems. They do not need a dedicated compliance team, as a documented policy owned by a single named person suffices for most companies under 50 people. The safe harbor path for most SMEs involves three steps: using a vendor who can confirm their EU AI Act compliance posture, documenting usage through a GPAI register and human oversight notes, and adding transparency notices to satisfy Article 50. Enforcement will focus on transparency failures, with national market surveillance authorities having power to issue fines reaching 3% of global annual turnover. Beyond regulatory risk, enterprise customers and public-sector clients will increasingly require documented AI compliance as a procurement condition. The regulation includes a proportionality principle meaning obligations must be applied in a manner proportionate to the size and nature of the entity, though this reduces administrative burden rather than the core transparency duties. Companies should treat this deadline as an operational necessity rather than a checkbox exercise, as the practical implications extend beyond compliance to maintaining business relationships with customers and partners who will increasingly demand evidence of AI governance.

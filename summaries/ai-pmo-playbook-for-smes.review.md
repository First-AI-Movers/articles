## 500-word summary

This article provides a comprehensive playbook for how growing companies can build a lightweight AI programme management function that creates visibility, clear ownership, and measurable business results without importing enterprise-level overhead. The article explains that most growing businesses hit an AI governance wall between their third and sixth pilot, when informal experiments multiply across departments and leadership cannot answer basic questions about what is running, who owns each initiative, and which ones are actually producing results. The article argues that a simple operating structure creates visibility and ownership without the overhead that smaller businesses cannot absorb, and emphasizes that AI activity without coordination does not fail all at once but degrades over time: ownership drifts, decisions stall, good results go unrecognised, and bad experiments keep running because nobody has defined stopping rules. The article outlines six things an AI PMO function should do well in a 20-to-80 person company: keep pilots visible through a shared register of every active experiment, clarify ownership with one named accountable person per pilot, standardize decision criteria so that the same questions apply to every pilot at review points, reduce tool sprawl by maintaining a visible inventory of AI tools across the business, support manager supervision by codifying what AI-assisted outputs look like when wrong and when human review is mandatory, and connect AI work to business proof through measurable outcomes like time saved or error rates reduced. The article provides a practical three-month sequencing guide: month one focuses on making the inventory real by discovering experiments, tools, and inconsistencies while creating a live pilot register with a complete tool inventory and named owners for every initiative; month two applies consistent review against six dimensions and structures manager supervision with role-specific guidance on output review and escalation paths; and month three makes decisions about which pilots created measurable value, which should scale, which should be redesigned with tighter scope, and which should stop. The article emphasizes that the programme function does not need additional headcount but needs five elements: one accountable owner with visibility and decision authority, one shared pilot register visible to leadership and updated at each review, one monthly review forum as a standing 60-to-90 minute session, one short approved-use baseline document defining approved tools for data categories and where human review is mandatory, and one escalation path for unexpected outputs or sensitive data concerns. Signs the function is working include leadership answering what AI pilots are running in under two minutes, new tool requests going through a lightweight approval step, pilots closing when they do not produce measurable value rather than running indefinitely, and managers describing their review responsibility without consulting documentation. The article notes that the function is not working when the primary output is status reporting, the register is only updated before meetings, and decisions keep being deferred.

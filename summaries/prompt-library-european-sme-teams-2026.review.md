## 500-word summary

This guide provides European SMEs with a step-by-step approach to building a shared AI prompt library, framed as one of the highest-leverage investments a 20-50 person company can make in 2026. The problem it solves is direct: team members using Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini independently reinvent the same prompts, wasting 30-40 minutes each on tasks like cash flow summaries that could be standardized. A prompt library captures what works, discards what does not, and transforms AI capability from an individual skill into a team asset. The guide recommends organizing by business function—communications, analysis, HR and people, finance and operations, and legal and compliance—rather than by AI tool, so users find prompts by what they need to do. Each template entry follows a consistent format: a short searchable name, a one-sentence use case, the full prompt text with placeholders in square brackets, a description of expected output, the AI tool it was tested on, and an owner for maintenance. Placeholders are described as the critical design decision—they should mark every piece of information that changes between uses. For storage, the guide recommends using platforms the team already uses: Google Workspace with a linked table of contents, Notion databases with filters, Microsoft 365 SharePoint pages, or Confluence spaces. The library should be readable by everyone but editable only by one or two designated maintainers per function. Four ready-to-use templates are provided: a meeting summary prompt, a job description draft, a supplier delay response email, and a cash flow variance note. Maintenance involves quarterly reviews with function leads—a 30-minute call is sufficient—to test templates against current AI versions, mark unreliable ones as under review, retire superseded ones, and add new validated prompts. The guide explicitly warns against using real personal data in template placeholders and reminds users that personal data should not be pasted into consumer AI tools without a signed Data Processing Agreement with that vendor. For SMEs starting out, the guide suggests a half-day workshop can produce a working first version with 15-20 templates, and recommends starting with one shared library rather than separate ones per department, splitting only if the library exceeds 60 templates or departments have significantly different governance requirements. The core value proposition is simple: rather than treating AI as a personal productivity hack that lives in each employee's browser, teams can systematize their best AI interactions so that the entire organization benefits from every experiment that works. This shifts AI from a tool that makes individuals more productive to infrastructure that makes the whole team more capable, regardless of each person's comfort level with prompting. By making prompts discoverable and reusable, the library also accelerates onboarding—new team members can immediately access validated templates rather than starting from zero. The guide acknowledges that not every prompt belongs in a shared library; highly specialized, one-off, or exploratory prompts may remain personal, but the library captures the 80% of use cases that are repetitive across the team. Testing templates quarterly against current AI versions ensures the library stays reliable as models evolve, since a prompt that worked well in 2025 may underperform in 2026 due to changes in model behavior or training data.

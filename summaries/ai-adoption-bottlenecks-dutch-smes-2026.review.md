## 500-word summary

AI adoption typically fails not because the model is weak, but because the organization is not designed to absorb the change. This problem is bigger than most leaders realize, particularly for firms with 50 to 250 employees in the Netherlands, where the 2025 Digital Decade report indicates smaller firms need more practical support to adopt advanced digital technologies. Dutch and global research increasingly confirms the same pattern: investment is rising but value remains uneven because workflows, enablement, and governance are not keeping up. Most companies diagnose AI adoption problems incorrectly. They assume the problem is one of employees needing more prompts, teams needing more licenses, models needing to get better, or IT needing to roll out tools faster. Those can matter but are rarely the real constraint. The real bottleneck is usually a combination of unclear workflow ownership, weak leadership alignment, generic training instead of role-based enablement, no governance for real-world use, and no measurement tied to business outcomes. This diagnosis aligns with what the market is already signaling. EY Netherlands frames successful AI adoption around mindset, skillset, and toolset, explicitly listing limited transformative vision, lack of employee skills, fragmented data, unclear use-case value, and lack of governance as core hurdles. AI Coalition 4 NL also says promising initiatives still fail early because organizations lack knowledge, skills, room for experimentation, and enough embedded ethics and legislation in development processes. Common adoption problems include a few power users getting value while most teams do not, leadership saying AI is strategic while managers treat it as optional, pilots looking good but nobody pointing to workflow-level ROI, people using public tools quietly outside governance, technical teams building while business teams do not change how they work, and adoption being wide in theory but shallow in practice. That last point matters more than it seems. OpenAI's 2025 enterprise AI report found a widening gap between frontier users and median users, with especially large usage differences in more advanced tasks. Organizations often mistake access for adoption while real value concentrates among a small minority of employees. The real sequence that works involves picking one workflow that matters rather than issuing broad department mandates, assigning one business owner to prevent adoption from fading, creating role-based enablement for people who will actually use and supervise the system, adding minimum viable governance early to reduce ambiguity and help teams use AI safely, and measuring behavior and business value together because if usage rises but outcomes do not, the workflow design is wrong. Most Dutch companies do not need another inspiration session; they need an adoption diagnosis, workflow prioritization, manager-level enablement, minimum viable governance, role-based training, and one or two measured wins. This represents the practical middle ground between everyone getting a license and building a giant AI transformation office. Once AI adoption stalls, the issue is rarely technical only—it becomes a cross-functional design problem involving leadership, workflows, learning, trust, measurement, and governance.

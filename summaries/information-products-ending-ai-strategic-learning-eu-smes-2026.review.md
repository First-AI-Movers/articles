## 500-word summary

The article contends that the long-dominant model of monetizing expertise through static information products—ebooks, courses, coaching programs—is reaching structural limits due to market saturation, AI acceleration, and shifts in search behavior. It references Eugene Schwartz's five stages of market sophistication, noting that information products have reached the final stage where brand is the primary differentiator and average products face diminishing returns regardless of marketing skill. Completion rate data starkly illustrates the problem: self-paced online courses typically achieve only 5–15% completion, with over half of enrollees never starting. In contrast, courses incorporating coaching, community, or interactive elements see completion rates above 70%, proving the gap lies in delivery format, not content quality. Three converging forces have commoditized information: volume saturation (anyone can create a course), AI acceleration (generative AI makes production easy and fast), and search/discovery shifts (people can query AI for personalized answers instantly, weakening the value of compiled information). The initial creator response—emphasizing authenticity—has itself become saturated and fails to overcome format fatigue. The path forward is not better information products but evolving beyond them entirely. The article advocates returning to the apprenticeship model at scale, enabled by AI. Unlike industrial-era lectures that standardized delivery, AI allows personalized, interactive guidance alongside each learner, correcting in real time and adapting to their situation. This shift from information products to learning experiences is critical: an information product delivers content to be consumed, while a learning experience guides action, provides feedback, and adapts to progress. Dan Koe's concept of 'evolved information products' exemplifies this: instead of a course with modules, an AI-powered interface where users interact with the creator's knowledge. Three modes are outlined: Learn (interactive conversations adapting to user confusion), Practice (AI evaluates attempts and refines approaches), and Create (guided implementation producing real outputs). Tools like Replit, Cursor, and Claude already allow non-programmers to build such applications through iterative conversation. The broader strategic principle extends beyond selling courses: client onboarding (interactive guides replacing documentation), internal training (AI coaching systems replacing manuals), customer support (AI trained on knowledge base solving specific problems), and consulting delivery (tools guiding step-by-step implementation). The competitive advantage equation emphasizes that your edge isn't doing what AI can't do—it's doing what only you would think to do with AI, rooted in specific knowledge gained from years of deep domain obsession. Combining that expertise with an existing audience and AI-enabled delivery creates a difficult-to-replicate position. An implementation framework is provided: Phase 1 (weeks 1–2) audits all static content expecting independent implementation; Phase 2 (weeks 3–4) maps the learning journey backward from demonstrated capability; Phase 3 (weeks 5–8) builds a minimal interactive pilot for one use case; Phase 4 (ongoing) expands and systematizes based on learnings. A timeline consideration notes that information products dominated for perhaps 15 years, but the interactive shift may last only 2–3 years before the next evolution, urging quick action and continuous adaptation. The article addresses the objection that this is just repackaging in an AI wrapper by arguing that the wrapper determines the interaction model, which determines outcomes—just as cloud infrastructure was dismissed as just a wrapper but fundamentally changed software. In conclusion, the shift from information to interactive experiences represents a fundamental change in how knowledge is transferred, leveraging AI to provide scalable, personalized guidance that mimics apprenticeship.

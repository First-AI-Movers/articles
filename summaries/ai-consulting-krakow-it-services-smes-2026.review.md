## 500-word summary

Krakow, Poland's second-largest IT hub after Warsaw, has developed a strong cluster of software companies serving both Polish and DACH clients, leveraging proximity to the German market and talent from AGH University of Science and Technology and Jagiellonian University. However, these companies face a unique dual-market compliance challenge: serving Polish clients under UODO and GDPR while simultaneously meeting German enterprise requirements that go beyond EU law. For Polish clients, the primary compliance obligations center on UODO enforcement priorities, including training data provenance (ensuring legal basis covers both original collection and AI training purposes), automated decision-making disclosures under GDPR Article 22 for employment and financial applications, and proper Data Processing Agreements with AI API vendors—particularly flagged for Polish companies using US-based AI services without adequate GDPR transfer mechanisms.

German enterprise clients add a commercially mandatory compliance layer that is not legally binding on Polish suppliers but determines contract renewal decisions. BSI IT-Grundschutz alignment is required for security architecture documentation, access control evidence, and incident response procedures. The Digital Operational Resilience Act applies from January 2025 to ICT providers classified as critical for financial services clients, imposing contractual provisions, audit rights, and operational resilience testing requirements. Additionally, German procurement teams increasingly require EU AI Act technical documentation and Declarations of Conformity for AI components ahead of the August 2026 enforcement date.

Two Polish national funding instruments support AI development. PARP administers EU structural fund programmes for SME digitization and R&D through Digital Poland and Smart Growth operational programmes. NCBR funds higher-risk R&D through Fast Track and collaborative research calls with universities, covering up to 80 percent of eligible R&D costs for SMEs. Both require clear separation of R&D from product development, with NCBR particularly suited for companies with genuine research content like novel explainability approaches or AI safety testing infrastructure.

The guide recommends a three-phase engagement model. Phase one involves a two to three-week compliance gap analysis mapping AI use cases against EU AI Act tiers, UODO obligations, and German client standards with prioritized remediation estimates. Phase two requires four to six weeks of architecture and documentation work for high-priority use cases, producing EU AI Act technical documentation, DPA updates, and UODO governance documentation. Phase three structures R&D elements for PARP or NCBR funding while implementing ongoing compliance monitoring for new client contracts and AI components before deployment.

For gaming companies, AI applications like procedural content generation and NPC behavior generally fall outside EU AI Act high-risk categories, though GDPR still applies to player data. The German client compliance layer only becomes relevant when licensing technology to German enterprise clients outside gaming.

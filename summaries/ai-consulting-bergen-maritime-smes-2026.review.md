## 500-word summary

Bergen is Norway's maritime capital, home to shipowners, offshore service companies, port operators, ship management firms, and the technology vendors that serve them. For a sector historically operationally conservative due to safety and asset stakes, 2026 is presenting a different kind of pressure: regulatory and commercial forces that make AI adoption no longer a strategic option but an operational requirement.

Two regulatory frameworks are directly reshaping data requirements for Bergen maritime SMEs. FuelEU Maritime came into force in January 2025, requiring shipping companies operating within EU ports to measure, report, and progressively reduce the greenhouse gas intensity of their vessel operations. For a company running five to fifteen vessels on routes touching EU ports, this is a current data collection and reporting obligation, not a future compliance problem. The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism adds further reporting requirements for companies involved in cargo including steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, or electricity—requiring supply chain emissions data most SMEs are not currently capturing.

The AI that matters for Bergen maritime SMEs is not conversational. It is about emissions data that regulators will require to be accurate and auditable, vessel performance that determines whether a ship earns or loses money on a route, and maintenance cycles that are currently driven by schedules rather than actual equipment condition. These are problems where AI delivers measurable operational value—and where getting the implementation wrong creates real liability.

Three areas matter most for day-to-day performance. Vessel performance optimisation uses historical voyage data—speed, weather routing, fuel consumption, cargo load—to identify patterns that reduce fuel use per tonne-mile. For a vessel burning 20-30 tonnes of fuel per day, a 3-5% efficiency improvement is material at current fuel prices. Predictive maintenance shifts maintenance scheduling from fixed intervals to condition-based triggers, combining sensor data from engines, pumps, and auxiliary systems with historical failure records. For an offshore service vessel, an unplanned technical stop can cost NOK 500,000 to 1,500,000 in downtime and emergency repair. Port and logistics coordination assists with scheduling, berth allocation, cargo sequencing, and multi-stop voyage planning.

Data sovereignty matters more in maritime than in other sectors. Vessel performance data, route efficiency data, and client cargo patterns are genuinely proprietary. The difference between how efficiently your fleet runs a particular route and how a competitor runs the same route is commercially valuable information. Bergen maritime SMEs evaluating AI vendors need to apply stricter data governance questions: Where is the data stored? Who has access to it in the vendor organisation? Can the vendor use aggregated data for model training that benefits competitors?

The appropriate framing for AI consulting in this context is risk reduction rather than innovation acceleration. The risk of non-compliance with emissions reporting obligations, the risk of unplanned maintenance stops, the risk of signing a vendor contract that exposes proprietary fleet data, and the risk of implementing a system that operations staff do not trust and therefore do not use. A structured AI readiness assessment covering compliance gap analysis, data infrastructure review, vendor shortlist against sovereignty criteria, and phased implementation recommendation typically takes four to eight weeks.

## 500-word summary

The NIS2 Directive (Directive 2022/2555/EU) became effective across EU member states in October 2024, marking a significant expansion of mandatory cybersecurity obligations for European companies. NIS2 divides covered organizations into two categories: essential entities, which are large organizations (250+ employees or €50M+ annual turnover) in critical sectors such as energy, transport, banking, financial market infrastructure, health, drinking water, digital infrastructure, and public administration; and important entities, which are mid-sized organizations (50-249 employees or €10-49M turnover) in a broader set of 18 sectors including postal services, waste management, chemicals, food production, manufacturing, and digital providers such as online marketplaces, cloud services, and search engines. A 30-person software company or 45-person logistics firm in covered sectors may now be an important entity without realizing it. Under NIS2 Article 21, important entities must implement 10 mandatory cybersecurity measures: risk analysis and information system security policies, incident handling procedures, business continuity and disaster recovery, supply chain security policies, secure development practices, policies for assessing cybersecurity effectiveness, basic cyber hygiene and staff training, cryptography and encryption policies, human resources security and access controls, and multi-factor authentication for critical systems and remote access. The directive introduces strict incident reporting timelines: 24 hours for early warning notification to the national competent authority, 72 hours for initial severity assessment, and one month for a final report with root cause analysis and remediation measures. Administrative fines for important entities reach up to €7 million or 1.4% of global annual turnover. For SMEs in scope, the practical starting approach is a five-step gap analysis: first, identify which IT systems constitute network and information systems covered by NIS2; second, designate a senior owner (CTO, CISO, or fractional security lead) with management body accountability for approving cybersecurity measures; third, audit existing controls against the 10 Article 21 requirements; fourth, register with the national competent authority; and fifth, build incident response procedures with centralized log monitoring and escalation paths to meet the 24-hour early warning requirement. Organizations with existing GDPR incident response processes have a foundation for NIS2 compliance, though the 24-hour NIS2 timeline is shorter than GDPR's 72-hour requirement. For organizations deploying AI tools in covered network or information systems, NIS2 requirements intersect with the EU AI Act through supply chain security for AI vendors, incident handling for AI system failures, and access control for AI system administration. Micro enterprises with fewer than 10 employees and annual turnover under €2 million are generally excluded regardless of sector. The directive applies to both public and private sector entities, with member states required to transpose NIS2 into national law and designate national competent authorities responsible for oversight and enforcement. Organizations should begin assessing their entity status, mapping their sector classification, and reviewing current cybersecurity measures against Article 21 requirements to ensure compliance before enforcement begins.

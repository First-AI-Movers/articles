The EU AI Act creates compliance obligations for accounting and professional services firms deploying AI tools, with full enforcement beginning August 2026 for general-purpose AI systems. The regulation uses a risk-based classification system: general-purpose AI (minimal deployer obligations), Annex III high-risk systems (full Article 26 obligations), and prohibited AI (social scoring, subliminal manipulation). For high-risk tools like AI-assisted employee performance scoring or hiring systems, firms must designate human reviewers, monitor input data quality, maintain incident logs, and complete basic risk assessments. Most AI tools used by SMEs fall into the general-purpose category, including automated bookkeeping (Dext, Vic.ai), tax preparation assistants, and document drafting tools. ## 500-word summary

The EU AI Act establishes a comprehensive regulatory framework that applies to accounting firms, management consultancies, and professional services SMEs deploying AI tools across Europe, with enforcement beginning in August 2026 for general-purpose AI systems and earlier for high-risk categories already in force. The regulation employs a three-tier risk classification system that determines the scope of deployer obligations. General-purpose AI systems, which include large language models used for document drafting, client communication templates, research assistance, and meeting summaries, carry minimal deployer obligations such as documenting usage in GDPR Article 30 records, maintaining basic usage registers, and providing transparency to clients when AI assists in producing deliverables that materially affect client expectations under Article 50. Annex III high-risk AI systems impose full Article 26 deployer obligations on professional services firms. This category notably includes AI tools used for employment and worker management under Category 5, such as systems that assess employee performance, determine compensation, or make hiring decisions, as well as Category 8 systems used in the administration of justice and democratic processes, which may apply to legal support tools that prepare or recommend legal arguments. For these high-risk systems, firms must designate a specific person for human oversight who reviews AI outputs before decisions are finalized, ensure input data monitoring with documented data sources, maintain incident logs recording incorrect or contested outputs, and complete basic risk assessments covering what the tool does, who it is affected, what could go wrong, and mitigation measures. The article addresses specific scenarios common to accounting firms: automated bookkeeping tools like Dext, Botkeeper, and Vic.ai generally fall into the minimal-risk category unless used to flag employee expense claims affecting employment decisions; tax preparation and VAT automation tools are generally-purpose AI since accountants review and approve filings; AI-assisted audit sampling and anomaly detection should be treated as research tools rather than decision tools with human auditor review of all flagged items; and KYC/AML due diligence tools sit at the intersection of EU AI Act, GDPR, and the 6th Anti-Money Laundering Directive, requiring both a GDPR Data Processing Agreement and AML compliance documentation. The six-step compliance checklist provides a practical implementation path: inventory all AI tools including embedded features in existing software (firms typically find 8-15 tools), classify each tool by risk category, complete deployer documentation for high-risk tools, verify DPA coverage for all GDPR-regulated tools, update client engagement letters with AI disclosure clauses, and assign annual review dates. The FAQ clarifies that small firms under 10 employees have the same Article 26 obligations but can apply proportionality principles under Article 9(7), that AI writing tools used for internal drafting with professional review require no specific EU AI Act action beyond basic documentation, that enforcement fines reach EUR 15 million or 3% of global turnover, and that AI tool usage may need disclosure in ISAE 3402 or SOC 2 control matrices.

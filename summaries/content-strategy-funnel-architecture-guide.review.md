## 500-word summary

The article makes the case that websites fail to convert traffic not because of poor writing but because of missing funnel architecture. The fundamental problem is that most websites have one content type—blog posts that all sound the same, service pages that all say the same thing—without any structure connecting what visitors need to what they actually see. A visitor searching "what is AI automation" (researching) is fundamentally different from someone searching "AI automation consultant Netherlands" (ready to buy), and serving them the same content loses both. The author identifies three distinct stages of buyer intent that require different content approaches. At the top of the funnel, researchers have a problem they're trying to understand but aren't looking for solutions yet. They search using "what is" and "how does" patterns, want education without sales pressure, and need definitions, frameworks, and context. They do not want service pitches or calls to book meetings—those lose them instantly. In the middle of the funnel, evaluators understand their problem and are actively comparing options. They search using "best," "compare," and "versus" patterns, need decision-making frameworks and case studies showing results, and want evidence that the provider understands their specific situation. At the bottom of the funnel, buyers have decided they need a solution and are looking for the right provider. They search with location-specific and transactional queries, need clear service descriptions, pricing clarity, and an easy path to conversation. The article demonstrates how mixing these stages kills conversion. When a blog post about "What is AI automation" ends with "Book a consultation today," it violates the reader's intent—they came to learn, and the sales pitch makes them leave. When service pages spend 500 words explaining what AI governance means before describing the actual offering, it wastes the buyer's time—they came ready to evaluate, and the textbook-first approach sends them to a competitor who gets to the point. The solution is a four-layer content architecture. The Intel Section (top of funnel) houses educational content to capture search traffic from people researching problems, with soft CTAs like newsletter subscriptions or framework downloads. The Methodology Section (middle of funnel) explains how the business thinks and works, converting researchers into evaluators with medium-pressure CTAs. The Solutions Section (bottom of funnel) contains clear service descriptions, specific deliverables, and direct CTAs for ready buyers. The Network Section builds trust through partners and vendors. Internal linking is critical: educational articles should link to methodology, methodology should link to services, and service pages should have prominent, easy contact paths. The audit process involves classifying every content page by intent, checking if CTAs match intent stages, mapping internal links, and identifying gaps. The implementation sequence prioritizes bottom-of-funnel content first (service pages converting ready buyers), then middle-of-funnel (methodology content as the bridge), then top-of-funnel (educational content capturing search traffic), and finally connecting everything with internal links. The author uses Core Ventures and First AI Movers as examples of this architecture in practice, with each layer having a distinct purpose and each content type having its place.
